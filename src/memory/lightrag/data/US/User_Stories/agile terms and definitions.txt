User Story - Definition of Ready (DoR)
1. Story is Well-Formed
o Follows INVEST criteria 
(Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable).
o Written in the format:
“As a [role], I want [activity] so that [business value].”
2. Acceptance Criteria are Defined
o Clear, testable, and aligned with SAFe's Built-In Quality.
o Use BDD format (Given / When / Then) where possible.
3. Business Value and Context are Clear
o Story is traceable to a Feature in the Program Backlog.
o Stakeholder and customer value are understood and documented.
4. Capacity and Enablers Considered
o Any necessary Enabler Stories (e.g., for architecture, compliance, or infrastructure
) are identified and planned.
o The story fits within the team Iteration capacity.
5. Dependencies Identified and Addressed
o Cross-team or cross-ART dependencies are visible on the Program Board.
o Coordination with System Architect, Product Management, or other teams is 
complete. 
6. No Blockers Remain
o Story has no unresolved risks or impediments that would prevent it from being pu
lled into the Iteration.
7. UX/Design/Compliance Inputs Reviewed (if applicable)
o UI mockups, regulatory constraints, and design guidelines are attached if relevant.
8. Acceptance by Product Owner
o Product Owner has reviewed and accepted the story into the Team Backlog as par
t of backlog refinement.
9. Testable and confirmed by QA
o Defined acceptance criteria ensure testability.
o Quality assurance or test engineers confirm they can validate the story within the
sprint.
10. Effort is Estimated
o The team has provided a story point estimate via Planning Poker or other consens
us-based estimation.
Feature - Definition of Ready (DoR)
A Feature is considered “Ready” to be pulled into a Program Increment (PI) or 
implemented by an Agile Release Train (ART) when it meets the following criteria:
1. Well-Defined Title and Description
a. Feature has a clear, concise title.
b. Description uses the standard format:
c. “As a [role], I want [capability], so that [benefit].”
2. Business Value is Identified
a. The Feature clearly articulates business or customer value.
b. Associated with a business driver, OKR, or strategic theme.
3. Acceptance Criteria are Defined
a. Acceptance criteria follow Given/When/Then or equivalent format.
b. Must be testable and verifiable at the feature level.
4. Sized to Fit Within a PI
a. Can be completed within a single Program Increment (typically 2–3 
months).
b. Further decomposed into smaller user stories during backlog refinement.
5. Prioritized and Approved by Product Management
a. Product Management has reviewed and prioritized the feature in the 
Program Backlog.
b. Business Owners or stakeholders have reviewed and support the value 
case.
6. Dependencies are Identified
a. Cross-team and cross-ART dependencies are documented.
b. Coordination with external systems, teams, or vendors is planned.
7. Non-Functional Requirements are Considered
a. Performance, security, compliance, and scalability constraints are noted 
if relevant.
8. Design and UX Artifacts are Available (if applicable)
a. UX wireframes, mockups, or prototypes are provided and approved by 
stakeholders.
b. System Architect/Engineering has reviewed technical feasibility if 
applicable.
9. Test and Validation Approach is Agreed
a. Preliminary test strategy is defined.
b. Teams agree the feature is testable and can be validated within the PI.
10.Feature is Linked to Capabilities or Enablers (if applicable)
a. The Feature is traceable to higher-level Capabilities or Epics.
b. Supporting Enabler Features are identified and aligned.
11.Effort is Estimated
a. T-shirt size or story point estimate provided by involved teams or ARTs.
12.Feature is Socialized Across Teams
a. Feature has been reviewed in backlog refinement or PI planning prep 
meetings.
b. ART leadership (RTE, Product Manager, System Architect) is aware and 
aligned. 
Epic - Definition of Ready
An Epic is a large, cross-cutting business or technical initiative that typically spans 
multiple Agile Release Trains (ARTs) and Program Increments (PIs). It must meet the 
following criteria before it is approved for implementation:
1. Epic Hypothesis Statement is Clearly Defined
a. Follows the Epic format:
b. “We believe that [capability] will result in [measurable benefit]. We will 
know we are successful when [leading indicator or KPI].”
2. Business Outcome and Strategic Fit Are Identified
a. Clearly aligned to:
b. Strategic themes
c. OKRs
d. Portfolio vision
e. Expected business outcome is measurable and articulated.
3. Epic Owner Is Assigned
a. A designated Epic Owner is responsible for shepherding the epic through 
the Portfolio Kanban system.
4. Initial Lean Business Case Is Created
a. Contains:
a. Problem statement
b. Business and customer impact
c. Success criteria
d. Cost of delay
e. Forecasted MVP scope and cost
b. Approved or in review by Lean Portfolio Management (LPM).
5. Enablers and Dependencies Identified
a. Supporting Enabler Epics or architectural work are noted.
b. Cross-ART or cross-vendor dependencies documented.
c. Reviewed by Enterprise or Solution Architect if applicable.
6. Investment Guardrails and Budget Impact Understood
a. Complies with portfolio-level guardrails (e.g., budget caps, capacity 
allocations).
b. Initial cost estimate fits within portfolio boundaries.
7. MVP Scope Identified
a. Clear definition of the Minimum Viable Product (MVP).
b. Describes the smallest value delivery that can validate the hypothesis.
8. Initial Capability and Feature Breakdown Started
a. High-level Capabilities and Features identified or outlined for 
decomposition.
b. Prepared for Value Stream coordination or WSJF estimation.
9. Review and Agreement from Key Stakeholders
a. Relevant stakeholders have reviewed and aligned on:
a. Value hypothesis
b. MVP
c. Risks and assumptions
10.Approved to Enter the Portfolio Backlog
a. Ready to transition from Funnel → Analyzing → Backlog stage in 
the SAFe Portfolio Kanban.
b. Reviewed in Portfolio Sync or Epic Review Board. 
INVEST Criteria for Story Writing
INVEST Breakdown
Letter Stands for Breakdown
I Independent The story should be self-contained. It should not depend on another 
story to be developed or delivered.
N Negotiable The story is not a contract; it's a placeholder for conversation and can 
be refined or adjusted during discussions.
V Valuable The story must deliver value to the customer, end user, or business 
(even if it's an internal technical story).
E Estimable The team must be able to estimate the effort required, even if only 
roughly. Lack of clarity or dependencies hinders this.
S Small The story should be small enough to be completed in a single sprint
T Testable There must be clear acceptance criteria so the team can test and 
confirm the story is complete and works as expected
Deep Dive into Each Element
I – Independent
o Stories should not be tightly coupled.
o This enables parallel development and flexible prioritization.
o Avoid: “Story B can’t be started until Story A is done.”
o Tip: If stories are dependent, consider combining them or adjusting their scope.
N – Negotiable
o Stories should invite collaboration and refinement.
o They are not detailed specs — they are conversation starters.
o Stakeholders, developers, and testers refine stories together during backlog grooming 
or planning.
o Tip: Leave room for refinement, especially in early stages.
V – Valuable
o The story must clearly explain who benefits and how.
o Helps Product Owners prioritize work by ROI.
o Even technical stories should map to business value (e.g., improving performance, 
enabling faster delivery).
o Tip: Use the format:
o “As a [role], I want [story] so that [benefit].”
E – Estimable
o The team must have enough information to size the story.
o If not, it may need spikes, prototypes, or clarification.
o Estimation helps with capacity planning and velocity tracking.
o Tip: If you can't estimate it, you probably don’t understand it well enough yet.
S – Small
o Stories should be small enough to fit into a single sprint.
o If too large, break into vertical slices (e.g., UI + backend + integration for one use 
case).
o Tip: A typical story should take 1–3 days of effort. If it's longer, split it while 
preserving value.
T – Testable
o There must be clear acceptance criteria.
o This enables definition of done, QA testing, and stakeholder validation.
o Ideally written using Given/When/Then or a checklist.
o Tip: If it can’t be tested, it can’t be considered done.
Behavior Driven Development (BDD)
• What is Behavior-Driven Development (BDD)?
o Behavior-Driven Development (BDD) is an Agile software development practice 
that emphasizes collaboration between business stakeholders, developers, and 
testers to define the expected behavior of a system using concrete examples in 
plain language.
o BDD extends Test-Driven Development (TDD) by shifting the focus from testing 
implementation to specifying behavior, ensuring alignment between technical 
teams and business needs.
• Core Concepts of BDD
o Ubiquitous Language
▪ A common vocabulary used by developers, testers, and business analysts.
▪ Based on the domain model and used consistently in requirements, code, 
and tests.
o Examples and Scenarios
▪ Behavior is described through concrete, real-world examples.
▪ These examples are formatted into structured scenarios using Gherkin 
syntax.
o Executable Specifications
▪ The behavior examples become automated tests.
▪ They serve as both documentation and regression tests.
BDD Process (End-to-End)
1. Discovery – Understand Desired Behavior
a. Conducted as a collaborative discussion (often called a “Three Amigos” meeting).
b. Participants: Product Owner (or BA), Developer, Tester.
c. Goal: Clarify requirements and uncover edge cases.
d. Output: Shared understanding + candidate scenarios.
2. Formulation – Write Scenarios
a. Scenarios are expressed in Gherkin syntax, which uses:
Feature: <high-level capability>
Scenario: <specific case>
Given <precondition>
When <action or event>
Then <expected outcome>
3. Automation – Make It Executable
a. The Given/When/Then steps are mapped to code functions using BDD tools.
b. Each step is tied to actual system behavior (methods or service calls).
c. Tools parse the Gherkin file and execute the mapped step definitions.
4. Development – Write Code to Pass Scenarios
a. Developers implement just enough code to make the scenario pass.
b. Tests drive the design of the system by focusing on desired behavior
5. Validation and Continuous Integration
a. BDD scenarios are integrated into the CI/CD pipeline.
BDD in SAFe (Scaled Agile Framework)
1. BDD complements Agile Teams, ARTs, and PI Planning by:
2. Supporting Built-In Quality.
3. Ensuring that User Stories and Enablers are validated early.
4. Enhancing Test-First and Continuous Delivery pipelines.
5. Can be tied to Acceptance Test-Driven Development (ATDD).
Weighted Shortest Job First (WSJF)
WSJF is a prioritization model used to sequence jobs (e.g., Features, Capabilities, Epics) based 
on the cost of delay (CoD) and the duration it takes to complete them. It helps Agile teams and 
organizations deliver the maximum economic benefit in the shortest possible time.
It is calculated using the following formula:
WSJF = Cost of Delay / Job Duration
Where:
o Cost of Delay (CoD) = User/Business Value + Time Criticality + Risk Reduction and/or 
Opportunity Enablement
o Job Duration = Estimated size or effort (e.g., in story points or team weeks)
Breakdown of WSJF Components
1. User/Business Value
a. How much value the job delivers to the customer or business
b. Often scored on a relative scale (e.g., Fibonacci 1,2,3,5,8,13,21,…)
2. Time Criticality
a. How important it is to deliver the job soon (e.g., regulatory deadline, market window)
3. Risk Reduction / Opportunity Enablement
a. Will this job reduce future risk or enable new business opportunities?
Together, these three form the numerator: Cost of Delay
4. Job Size (Duration)
a. Estimate of the effort required to complete the work
b. Should be consistent across jobs (e.g., story points, team weeks)
c. Smaller jobs generally get a higher WSJF (other factors equal)
Normalized Story Points Definition
Normalized Story Points are a relative estimation technique used by Agile teams to size 
user stories based on effort, complexity, and risk — standardized across teams using a 
shared baseline. When using the Fibonacci sequence and defining 1 Story Point = 1 ideal 
day, the scale provides a structured way to estimate work based on increasing uncertainty.
Effort Mapping (1 SP = 1 Day)
Story Points Equivalent Effort Typical Use Case
1 1 Day Very simple task, trivial logic
2 2 Days Straightforward story with low risk
3 3 Days Moderate complexity, no major unknowns
5 5 Days (1 Week) More complex, may involve integration or testing
8 8 days (2 Weeks) Cross-functional, may include edge cases or rework
13 13+ days (3 Weeks) High complexity or many unknowns; often a 
candidate for splitting
21 21+ days (1 PI) Typically, too large — should be decomposed
Relative Story Points Definition
Relative Story Point Estimation is a method used by Agile teams to estimate the effort, 
complexity, and risk of a user story by comparing it to other stories — rather than assigning 
absolute time values.
When using the Fibonacci sequence (1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21), each number reflects a nonlinear increase in size and uncertainty, helping teams manage complexity effectively.
Fibonacci Sequence for Agile Estimation
1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21
These values are used because:
o They reflect increasing uncertainty as story size grows.
o The gap between values grows, discouraging over-precision.
o They encourage breaking down large work items.
What "Relative" Means
Rather than assigning absolute time (e.g., hours/days), teams compare stories:
o "Is this story twice as complex as that 3-point story? Then it's probably a 5."
o "This one is about the same size as another 2-point story."
o This method relies on team calibration and collective estimation, not individual 
opinions.

Design Thinking Definition
Design Thinking is a human-centered, iterative problem-solving approach used to 
understand user needs, redefine problems, and create innovative solutions. It emphasizes 
empathy, creativity, and experimentation to solve complex challenges in a user-focused 
way.
Design Thinking integrates the needs of people, the possibilities of technology, and the 
requirements for business success.
Core Principles of Design Thinking
o Human-centered: Focus on real user needs.
o Iterative: Continuously refine ideas and solutions.
o Collaborative: Involves cross-functional teams and stakeholders.
o Experimental: Encourages rapid prototyping and learning from failure.
Design Thinking Process Steps
While there are multiple models, the most commonly used (from Stanford d.school and 
IDEO) includes 5 stages:
1. Empathize
Goal: Understand users, their environment, and their challenges.
o Engage with users through interviews, observation, shadowing.
o Discover emotional drivers, pain points, and hidden needs.
o Avoid assumptions — seek deep empathy.
Tools: User interviews, empathy maps, journey mapping
2. Define
Goal: Clearly articulate the problem to be solved.
o Synthesize insights gathered during empathy.
o Frame the problem as a point of view (POV) statement:
o “[User] needs a way to [need] because [insight].”
Tools: Problem statements, affinity diagrams, “How Might We” questions
3. Ideate 
Goal: Generate a wide range of creative solutions.
o Encourage divergent thinking — quantity over quality first.
o Use brainstorming, mind mapping, or sketching to explore ideas.
o No judgment — build on each other’s ideas.
Tools: Brainstorming, SCAMPER, mind maps
4. Prototype 
Goal: Build low-fidelity versions of ideas to explore and validate them.
o Create rough, fast, inexpensive models of solutions.
o Could be paper sketches, wireframes, mockups, or physical models.
o The aim is to learn, not to perfect.
Tools: Paper prototypes, clickable mockups, 3D models
5. Test 
Goal: Gather feedback from real users to refine the solution.
o Observe users interacting with the prototype.
o Ask open-ended questions, look for pain points or confusion.
o Refine based on feedback — possibly return to earlier steps.
Tools: Usability tests, A/B testing, feedback surveys
Iterative Nature
Design Thinking is non-linear. You may jump between steps:
o Go back to Empathize after a failed test.
o Reframe the Define phase after deeper insights emerge.
o Rapidly prototype and test to validate multiple ideas.
Use Cases of Design Thinking
o Product and service innovation
o Customer experience redesign
o Business model development
o Organizational change initiatives
Persona Definition
A persona is a semi-fictional representation of a specific type of user or customer based on 
real-world data, research, and behavior patterns. Personas help teams understand the 
goals, needs, motivations, pain points, and behaviors of their target audience.
They are commonly used in:
o User experience (UX) design
o Product development
o Marketing
o Agile frameworks (e.g., SAFe, Scrum, Design Thinking)
Key Characteristics of a Persona
Element Description
Name A realistic name that personalizes the profile
Demographics Age, location, job title, income, education, etc.
Goals and Objectives What the persona is trying to achieve
Pain Points Frustrations, blockers, or challenges
Behaviors How they interact with your product or service
Tools/Devices Platforms or devices they commonly use
Quotes or Mindset Key expressions or attitudes that capture their thinking
Example Persona (for a productivity app)
Name: Sarah Martinez
Role: Project Manager
Age: 34
Goals:
o Keep team projects on schedule
o Reduce meeting time
o Improve cross-team visibility
Pain Points:
o Tool overload
o Difficult onboarding for new team members
o Poor integration with reporting systems
Quote:
"I need tools that help my team get work done, not add more work."
Why Personas Matter
o Build empathy with real users
o Drive design and development decisions
o Guide feature prioritization and product roadmaps
o Improve marketing messaging and user onboarding
o Align teams around a shared understanding of the user
Best Practices
o Base personas on qualitative and quantitative research (not assumptions)
o Keep them focused and realistic (avoid generic catch-all personas)
o Use multiple personas if your product serves different user types
o Update personas periodically as you gather more data
